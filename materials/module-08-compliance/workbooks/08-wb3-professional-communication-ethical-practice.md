## Section 1. Handling complaints

**Overview**

Not all complaints are the same
Some may be easily resolved with clear communication, while others need formal action.
Important: Always escalate serious, legal, or unresolved complaints to your licensee or agent-in-charge.
Lesson Summary

### 1.1 Responding to complaints

Best practice
Use this checklist to guide your approach.
Stay calm and respectful — never argue or react emotionally.
Listen without interrupting — allow the person to fully explain.
Acknowledge their concern without admitting fault. For example, “I'm sorry to hear you're feeling this way — I appreciate you raising it.”
Clarify what outcome they’re seeking.
Summarise back the key points to confirm understanding.
Take clear notes — include date, time, and key details.
Explain the complaints process clearly and calmly.

Encourage formal submission (for serious complaints)
Guide the person to provide a written complaint that includes:
a clear summary of the issue, including relevant dates and interactions
any supporting documentation (e.g. emails, contracts, photographs)
a statement of what outcome they are hoping for.
Good communication doesn’t mean having all the answers — it means making the person feel heard, respected, and taken seriously.
What not to do when handling complaints
Even with the best intentions, a poor response can escalate a complaint. Avoid these common mistakes:
Trying to resolve a serious or legal complaint yourself — especially if it involves you.
Getting defensive, emotional, or dismissive.
Encouraging someone to go directly to LGIRS without first trying internal resolution.
 Note: LGIRS will usually only investigate after the agency has had an opportunity to respond.
If a complaint is received in writing
Take immediate and professional action:
Forward it straight to your licensee or agent-in-charge.
	
Begin gathering relevant records (e.g. diaries, emails, contracts).
	
Respond quickly — delays increase the risk of formal escalation.
Final tip
Even if the issue can’t be resolved immediately, a respectful and professional response often makes the difference between a manageable complaint and a formal escalation to the regulator (LGIRS).

### 1.2 Explaining consumer rights and responsibilities

As a representative, you may need to explain consumer rights under the Australian Consumer Law (ACL) 
Especially when a complaint involves services provided by your agency.
Consumer guarantees for services (up to $40,000)
Consumers are entitled to expect services that:
are provided with reasonable care and skill
are fit for purpose as discussed
are delivered in a reasonable timeframe
don’t cause avoidable loss or damage.
If these guarantees aren’t met the consumer may:
cancel or request the service be redone
have it done elsewhere and pass on costs
request a refund or compensation.
As a representative always:
be honest and clear in your communication
explain your agency’s complaint process
escalate to your licensee if a dispute arises.



When helping clients understand rights (e.g. privacy, disclosure, refunds)
Use plain, everyday language.
Follow up in writing (emails, brochures, fact sheets).
Ask clarifying questions — e.g. “Can I check that made sense?”
Encourage questions — show openness.
Offer next steps — outline options and timeframes clearly.
“As your agent, I must disclose any material facts — like zoning changes or past damage. I’ll confirm this in writing as well.”

### 1.3 Resolving complaints and ethical concerns

Handling complaints professionally protects your client, your agency, and your reputation
Key strategies:
Stay calm and respectful — even when the client is upset.
Listen fully — don’t interrupt.
Acknowledge their frustration.
Clarify the issue — ask follow-up questions.
Explain the complaints process clearly.
Confirm actions in writing.
Thanks for raising that. I’ll document this and pass it to our licensee. You’ll receive a written update within three business days.”

### 1.4 Summary

Final tip: Proactive communication = Fewer complaints
Clear, early communication is your best defence against misunderstanding and complaints. Make sure your clients:
understand their rights and responsibilities
know what to expect from your service
are aware of how to raise a concern if needed.

## Section 2. Professional communication

**Overview**

Professional communication isn’t just about what you say — it’s how you say it, and the non-verbal cues you send
In real estate, where trust and clarity matter, your tone, facial expressions, and body language can either:
reinforce professionalism, or
damage client confidence if inconsistent with your words.
Example: Saying “I understand your concern” while folding your arms or avoiding eye contact sends a mixed message.

### 2.1 Verbal communication strategies

Verbal communication is the foundation of how you interact with clients, colleagues, and third parties
Clear, confident speech supports professionalism, trust, and compliance.
Key strategies:
Use plain, professional language — avoid jargon unless you explain it.


Confirm understanding — e.g. “Would you like me to go over that again?”


Stay calm and respectful — especially when handling complaints or difficult topics.


Summarise key points — help clients retain important details.


Adapt to your audience — speak differently to a first-home buyer than a legal professional.
“Under the REBA Code, I need to give you all material facts about the property. That includes this drainage issue noted in the last inspection.”

### 2.2 Non-verbal communication strategies

Non-verbal cues 
Your tone, posture, gestures, and facial expression — can reinforce or undermine what you say.
Key strategies:
Maintain eye contact — shows attentiveness and honesty.


Use an open posture — avoid crossing arms or appearing closed off.


Smile appropriately — build rapport, but match the mood of the conversation.


Mirror the client’s pace and tone — to create comfort and trust.


Control your tone — respectful delivery reduces defensiveness.
If delivering difficult news (like a rejected offer), a calm voice, soft expression, and open posture can reassure the client that you're acting in their best interest.

### 2.3 Risks of poor communication

Miscommunication often occurs when words and body language don’t match
Examples include:
Saying “I understand” in a rushed tone or with closed-off body language
Giving a legal explanation while avoiding eye contact — may seem evasive or dishonest
Ethical communication means your body language should support your words — especially in sensitive conversations.

### 2.4 Summary

Ethical communication in everyday relationships
 Whether working with buyers, tenants, landlords, or external agencies, your communication should always:
	
be honest and accurate — never withhold material facts
respect confidentiality
maintain a neutral, respectful tone in all situations
clarify your role (e.g. “I’m acting for the seller”)
disclose any conflicts of interest — even if minor.
“I represent the seller, but I’m here to ensure you have all the information you need to make an informed decision.”

## Section 3. Ethics and compliance

**Overview**

Communication strategies to support ethical practice
Ethical real estate professionals don’t just follow the rules — they explain them clearly, handle concerns respectfully, and build trust through consistent, honest communication.

Ethics go beyond what is legal. They reflect your professional integrity and personal values. Your goal is not just to comply with the law, but to act in ways that are:
trustworthy
respectful
beyond reproach.
The best agents are remembered not just for what they did — but how they did it.

### 3.1 Navigating ethical or compliance gaps

When your values don’t align
Sometimes, a real estate representative’s personal values, understanding of the law, or level of compliance may not fully align with their agency’s expectations — or with industry standards set by LGIRS, REIWA, or the REBA Code of Conduct.

Misalignment may be highlighted through:
client complaints or negative feedback
poor online reviews or social media mentions
compliance inspections or audit findings
internal feedback from managers or team members. 
The key is to respond proactively and constructively, not defensively.
Sometimes, what’s “technically legal” may still feel ethically wrong
	
For example:
Pressuring a buyer to sign quickly.
Withholding a low offer from a seller to avoid a conjunctional split.
Promoting a property before verifying details.
You may also encounter situations where your values clash with those of a colleague, manager, or client. This is where ethical reflection becomes critical.
Ethical reflection questions
Ask yourself:
Would I feel comfortable explaining this decision in front of the regulator?
Would I feel proud if this action was public?
Am I acting in the best interests of all parties — not just myself?
Could this choice be seen as deceptive or unfair?
Ethical challenges: Real-world examples
Scenario
	
Ethical issue


Telling a buyer there’s another offer to pressure a higher bid
	
Misrepresentation


Pressuring a tenant to leave quickly so the landlord can raise rent
	
Exploiting power imbalance


Hiding the fact that the agent’s spouse owns the property
	
Conflict of interest

These behaviours might not always break the law — but they can result in reputational harm, complaints, and disciplinary action if they undermine public trust.
What ethical agents and agencies should do
A professional, compliance-focused agency should provide support when issues arise. This includes:
one-on-one coaching or mentoring
clear policies, procedures, and ethical guidelines
team discussions that explain not just what is expected, but why
clear direction on where to find help or more information
transparent consequences if non-compliance continues.
If this support isn’t available, ask for it. If necessary, reach out to external bodies like REIWA or LGIRS for guidance.

### 3.2 What you can do to self-correct or rebuild trust

Taking ownership is a sign of professionalism
Here’s how to move forward when you recognise a gap in your practice.
Identify and acknowledge the issue
Understand the root cause
Set a plan for improvement
Small, consistent changes over time show that you’re learning and growing — and build trust with your colleagues, clients, and regulator.

### 3.4 Conflicts of interest

The law requires written disclosure of conflicts and written client consent to continue acting (REBA Act s.64; Code Rules 8, 15, 16)
But ethics go further:
Disclosure does not remove the conflict.
Clients may not fully understand what they’re consenting to.
Continuing to act can still undermine your integrity and the client’s trust.
Case example – Conflict of interest in action
A representative discloses a relationship with the buyer and obtains written consent from the seller.
However:
The rep negotiates less assertively for the seller
	
Their loyalty is influenced by the buyer — a repeat investor client
	
They hope to secure future business from the buyer
Outcome: The seller later files a complaint with the regulator, alleging a breach of trust and poor representation.

### 3.5 Managing conflict

Best practice
Ethical professionals and agencies will:
decline to act when a conflict exists — even if disclosure is made
be proactive in identifying and avoiding perceived conflicts
always prioritise the client’s interests over future business opportunities.
Refer to Real Estate Bulletins 96, 101, and 124 at commerce.wa.gov.au.

### 3.6 How to know if you’re getting it right

Staying on top of your legal and ethical obligations as a real estate representative isn’t always black and white
Unlike sales targets or KPIs, ethical performance is harder to measure — it depends on your behaviour, judgement, and the feedback you receive.
Still, there are clear ways to track your progress — both formally and informally.
What your agent or licensee might do
A licensee who prioritises professionalism and compliance may track your performance using tools like:
random client follow-up calls
annual client satisfaction surveys
collecting informal feedback such as:
verbal comments from clients
social media mentions
concerns or complaints.
reviewing internal agency records:
file audits and compliance reports
documentation checks or corrective actions.
 Note: Not every agent or agency has time to monitor this consistently so it’s important to take responsibility for self-assessing your own performance.
Ways to self-assess your ethical and legal practice
You don’t have to wait for formal reviews. Use these practical strategies to reflect on your professional performance.

Final thought
Being ethical and compliant isn’t about being perfect — it’s about being self-aware, reflective, and willing to grow.
The best professionals stay curious, ask questions, and welcome feedback — even when it’s uncomfortable.

### 3.7 Summary

Ethics builds long-term success
Every real estate professional will face difficult decisions at some point. What sets ethical agents apart is how they respond when something goes wrong.
Taking responsibility, asking questions, and committing to improvement are signs of strength — not weakness.
Ethical professionals don’t just follow the rules — they reflect, question, and choose actions that uphold fairness, transparency and respect. In doing so, they:
Protect themselves from risk
	
Contribute to a positive industry culture 
	
Build a career based on trust and integrity
“Real integrity is doing the right thing, even when it’s hard, even when no one notices.”

## Section 4. Putting it all together

**Overview**

What you need to do
Real estate practice involves complex legal, ethical, and professional obligations. Staying informed, applying best practice, and making ethical decisions must become part of your daily routine — not just something you do when a problem arises.
Your core responsibilities as a real estate representative
You are expected to:
stay up to date with current legislation, regulations, and codes
monitor changes to relevant laws and industry standards
follow instructions from your agency and industry regulators
know where to find credible legal, ethical, and compliance advice.

### 4.1 Integrating law, best practice and ethics

There’s no one-size fits all checklist
But these strategies will help you stay on track.
Follow your agency’s policies and procedures.
Think critically about risk and compliance in your daily work.
Pay extra attention to high-risk areas, such as disclosure, trust money, and safety.
Be consistent — apply a cautious, professional, and thorough approach every time.
Remember: Compliance isn’t just about avoiding fines — it’s about protecting consumers and maintaining trust in the profession.

### 4.2 Compliance hierarchy

What to follow first
When making professional decisions, use this order of priority.


The law – Always meet legal obligations.


Best practice – Apply codes, policies, and professional standards.


Personal ethics – Let your values guide you but never override the law.


 Good intentions aren’t enough — you need the technical knowledge to do things right.

### 4.3 Staying up to date with legal and ethical changes

Agencies are responsible for providing updates, but you also have a personal duty to stay informed
Ways to stay current:
Read your agency’s compliance communications (e.g. emails, bulletins).


Attend mandatory and elective CPD each year.


Subscribe to trusted update sources:
LGIRS and Landgate bulletins
REIWA newsletters (if you’re a member)
Australian Institute of Business Brokers (AIBB), if relevant.
	
 Tip: Set a calendar reminder to check these sources each month — staying informed is part of being a professional.

You don’t need to be a lawyer — but you do need to be informed, proactive, and ethical.
The most effective real estate professionals don’t just meet the minimum standards.
They lead with professionalism, act with integrity, and protect their clients by staying sharp, compliant, and always learning.

### 4.4 Case study

When good intentions still breach the law
Scenario
A well-meaning representative is door knocking and invited in by a property owner. They have a positive conversation, and the owner agrees to list. The client hands over a cheque for advertising on the spot, and the rep starts marketing the property immediately.
The problem
Despite her best intentions, the representative breached consumer law by failing to follow the rules for unsolicited consumer contracts under the Australian Consumer Law (ACL) and the Fair Trading Act (WA).
What went wrong
Took payment on the spot.
Started work within the 10-day cooling-off period.
Failed to provide a compliant written agreement.
Cold calling rules (ACL quick guide)
When door knocking:
No payments or services before cooling-off ends.
Written contract must include required disclosures.
Only approach during legal hours (no Sundays or public holidays).
Respect “Do Not Knock” signs and leave if asked.
Permitted hours:
Weekdays: 9:00 am – 6:00 pm
Saturdays: 9:00 am – 5:00 pm
Sundays/Public holidays: Not permitted
Disclosure requirements
You must:
clearly identify yourself and your agency
explain your purpose
leave immediately if asked.
Contract compliance
	
Provide a written agreement with prescribed disclosure.
Include a 10-day cooling-off period.
No payments may be accepted, and no services delivered during the 10 days.
Clients may cancel verbally or in writing, without penalty.
If any part of the process breaches ACL/FTA rules, the cooling-off period is extended.
Key takeaways
Good ethics ≠ legal compliance.
Best practice and ethics must be supported by legal awareness.
Even if the client agrees, you must follow legal process.
Breaching ACL rules can void contracts and harm your reputation.
Before door knocking: Know your agency policy, carry compliant forms, and never take payment on the spot.
Breaching consumer protection laws can invalidate contracts and damage reputation.

### 4.5 Summary

Lead with ethics, communicate with purpose
Communication isn’t just a skill — it’s a responsibility. Throughout this workbook, you’ve explored how strong, ethical communication supports compliance, builds client trust, and reduces risk. As you continue your real estate career, remember that clear, respectful, and professional communication is a daily habit — not just a tool for difficult moments. Whether you’re guiding a client through legislation or resolving a concern, your words, tone, and actions reflect who you are as a professional. Stay curious, stay ethical, and always aim to communicate in a way that inspires trust and confidence.
