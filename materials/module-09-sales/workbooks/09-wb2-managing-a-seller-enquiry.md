## Section 1. Handling a new enquiry

### Overview

**The enquiry: Starting strong with the seller**

When a potential seller reaches out, it is an opportunity to set the tone for a professional, helpful and confident relationship. The workbook says that agents should prepare, research and be ready to make a strong first impression.

To deliver that first impression, it identifies two needs:

- a solid understanding of what to research; and
- a clear process for handling each enquiry professionally.

### Section 2. Preparing for a property appraisal – Overview

Great appraisals start well before pricing
This stage is about laying a clean foundation: confirm how the land is held, verify ownership and authority, run the right title and planning checks, and build a clear legal picture of the property. You’ll revisit the essentials—tenure, title formats, Torrens principles, and encumbrances—and practise documenting what you find in a way that’s accurate, compliant, and easy to explain to clients. By the end of this section, you’ll have a complete, risk-aware profile ready to support a defensible appraisal.

### 2.1 Understanding property title and legal descriptions

You may come across different title formats depending on the property's use and location
Types of landholding include:
Fee Simple Green Title 
Strata Title 
Survey-Strata Title 
Share Title 
Crown Land Lease 
Community and Leasehold Titles 
 
Tenure – How land is ‘held’ 
Tenure refers to the legal way in which a person or entity holds rights to occupy or use land in Australia. Although land is privately owned, under Australian law absolute ownership is not possible—all land is ultimately held under the Crown. 
Instead, individuals and organisations hold land through various legal tenures, with different rights and responsibilities. 
 
Fee Simple (Freehold) – The highest form of private ownership 
Although technically the Crown retains ultimate ownership of all land, fee simple (also called freehold) represents the most complete interest an individual can hold. 
 
Key features:
Unlimited duration – there’s no end date to the interest.
Freely transferable – can be sold, gifted, or left to heirs.
Exclusive use and enjoyment, unless limited by legal interests (e.g. easements or restrictive covenants).
Subject to lawful government action – e.g. compulsory acquisition for public infrastructure.

### 2.2 Title search

Before you can market or appraise a property, you need to verify who owns it and what legal interests apply
A title search is a vital legal step required before any property is listed for sale or lease. This starts with confirming the address and legal description, which you’ll usually get directly from the client. 
According to Rule 25 — Client identification verification of the REBA Code of Conduct, a title search must be completed before a property is listed. The Code states:
An agent or sales representative who receives instructions to offer real estate for sale must:
(a) promptly obtain a copy of the certificate of title for the real estate and refer to that copy for the name of the registered proprietor.
 
What a title search reveals
A title search provides critical legal information about the property, including:
type of title (e.g., Green Title, Strata Title, Survey Strata)
registered owner(s)
encumbrances such as:
mortgages
easements
caveats
other legal restrictions that may affect use or value.
 
Best practice
Don’t just obtain the title—read it carefully. Encumbrances and restrictions can significantly affect marketing, saleability, and client expectations.

### 2.3 Confirming ownership details

For legal compliance and risk management, ownership must be verified using appropriate sources and checks
For legal compliance and risk management, you must verify who owns the property and ensure they have the authority to sell. This isn’t just good practice — it’s a requirement under the REBA Code of Conduct. 
According to Rule 25 — Client identification verification: 
As soon as practicable after receiving the instructions and before a contract for that sale is executed, an agent or sales representative must make all reasonable efforts to verify  
(i) the identity of each person who claims to be, or to act for, a person who is to sell all or any of the real estate; and 
(ii) each person’s authority to sell the real estate, or to act for the person selling it. 
 
Co-ownership of land 
More than one person can hold legal interest in land. There are two main types of co-ownership recognised under the Transfer of Land Act 1893 (WA), Section 60:
Joint tenancy
Tenancy in common
 
Legal note on the term “tenant” 
In legal terms, a tenant refers to any person with a recognised interest in land—including owners. While we often associate the word with renters, the concept also applies to landholders under fee simple, lease, or Crown tenures. This reflects the enduring principle that the Crown is the ultimate owner of all land in Australia. 
 
Practical application: How to verify ownership 
Ownership type 
	
Verification method 


Individual ownership 
	
100-point ID check of the registered owner(s) 


Company ownership 
	
Conduct an ASIC search and verify that the directors are authorised to act 


Property owned by a Trust 
	
Request and review the trust deed to confirm the trustee’s authority to sell 
Best practice: Always document the steps you’ve taken to verify ownership and authority. Keeping accurate records protects you, your agency, and your client.

### 2.4 The Torrens System – Australia’s property framework

Most land in Australia is held under the Torrens Title System
This system is designed to make property ownership secure, simple, and transparent by relying on a central register, rather than old-fashioned paper deeds. 
 
Key principles of the Torrens System
Mirror Principle 
Curtain Principle 
Insurance Principle 
Indefeasibility Principle 
 
Exceptions and anomalies
Even under the Torrens System, some risks or gaps can exist. 
Fraudulent registration (e.g. someone impersonates a seller).
Easements not properly recorded or incorrectly described.
Clerical errors (e.g. wrong name entered on title).
Unregistered short-term leases (less than 3 years) – these still bind a new buyer.

### 2.5 Summary

Good practice
Always complete a title search before listing.
Verify ownership using appropriate legal documents.
Stay current with new title types as they’re introduced.

### Section 3. Regulatory and legislative requirements and risks – Overview

When appraising property, you're not just providing a professional opinion, you're operating within a legal and ethical framework that protects both consumers and the profession
You must follow key laws and codes that apply to how appraisals are carried out and how information is communicated to clients.

Key rules that apply to appraisals
The Real Estate and Business Agents and Sales Representatives Code of Conduct 2016 (commonly known as the REBA Code) sets out your professional responsibilities.

Risk areas and prevention tips

	Prevention tip

Use of disclaimers 
If you’re passing on information from a client (e.g. owner-supplied details about property features or improvements), use a disclaimer to clarify that the information has not been independently verified. 
However: 
A disclaimer does not remove your obligation to act diligently and investigate where appropriate (as required under the REBA Code). 
You are still liable if your lack of care leads to someone being misled.
	
Disclosure statements 
To reduce risk, it’s common practice to have the seller sign a disclosure statement confirming:
any material facts they’re aware of 
that they’ve shared all relevant information with you.
This protects both you and your agency and promotes honesty and transparency.

### Section 4. Conclusion

A well-managed enquiry is more than collecting information—it’s an opportunity to demonstrate professionalism, build trust, and establish your reputation in the market. By verifying ownership, understanding client goals, and preparing a thoughtful pre-listing approach, you can provide accurate, compliant advice while protecting yourself and your agency from legal or ethical risks. Apply the processes in this workbook consistently, and you’ll turn initial enquiries into strong client relationships, quality appraisals, and successful property outcomes.
 
Remember: every appraisal you prepare is a reflection of your expertise. Approach each one with care, clarity, and honesty, and you’ll strengthen your reputation as a reliable real estate professional who clients can depend on.
