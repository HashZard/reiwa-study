# Preparing for Appraisal

## Section 1. Engaging the client – Overview

The first face-to-face meeting with a client is a critical step in the appraisal process
How you engage the client sets the tone for trust, professionalism, and a clear understanding of the property’s value.
When meeting a prospective client:
encourage them to review the appraisal report in advance
guide them through it at your meeting using clear, jargon-free language
acknowledge and discuss their expectations of value
be prepared to reiterate your rationale as buyer or tenant feedback is received during the campaign.

## 1.2 Meeting the clients and inspecting the property

Your first face-to-face meeting with a prospective client is a key step in the appraisal process
It’s your chance to build trust, inspect the property, and deliver your market appraisal in a way that meets legal requirements and professional standards. 
​ 
Inspecting the property​ 
Before appraising or listing any property, you must inspect it in person. This ensures you meet legal standards, build trust with the owner, and understand what you’re offering to the market. 
Take care with verbal or phone appraisals. Under the REBA Code (Rule 26), if you express an opinion about market value, you must also provide it in writing, including: 
the stated opinion 
the reasons on which it's based 
supporting comparable sales or rental data (if available).
To meet your duty of care, always inspect the property in person before confirming any appraisal. 
Why inspect
What tools to bring 
How to conduct the inspection 
What to look for 
​ 
Defects and disclosure 
Material defects must be discussed, disclosed, and ideally addressed before listing.
Encourage the owner to fix visible or easily remedied issues.
For unresolvable concerns (e.g. structural cracks), suggest a building inspection report. 
Ask for a signed disclosure statement and include a disclaimer in the listing authority.
Do not list if the seller refuses to allow disclosure of material facts.
Tip: Unaddressed defects not only impact sale or rental price—they can slow down marketing and pose legal risks. 
​ 
The REBA Code Rule 24 states:​ 
Prior to the execution by a client of any contract relating to the sale or lease of any real estate or business the agent or sales representative must make all reasonable efforts to ascertain or verify all facts material to the transaction (the material facts) that a prudent agent or sales representative would ascertain or verify.


An agent or sales representative must promptly communicate a material fact to any person who may be affected by the material fact and appears to be unaware of it.​

## 1.3 Seller warranties

Seller warranties under the JFGC
Under Section 9 of the Joint Form of General Conditions for the Sale of Land (JFGC), sellers provide certain warranties. These include confirming that there are no: 
demands, debts, claims, or orders affecting the land
unresolved fencing dispute
Important: Make sure sellers understand these warranties. If they knowingly or unknowingly provide false information, they may be liable for any losses the buyer suffers.

## 1.4 Tenant access

Access to tenanted properties for appraisals 
Section 46 of the Residential Tenancies Act 1987 (RTA) does not specifically provide for property access solely for the purpose of appraisals.
However, access can be arranged under the following circumstances: 
As part of a routine quarterly inspection.
Where repairs are required and access is justifiable. 
By negotiated agreement with the tenant. 
When arranging access, agents must comply with legal requirements.
Provide reasonable notice (unless the tenant gives consent).


Negotiate alternative times if the suggested time is inconvenient.


Conduct inspections during reasonable hours: 
Monday to Friday: 8am – 6pm 
Saturday: 9am – 5pm 
Or at another time agreed with the tenant
Inspect on a reasonable number of occasions only.
Keep the inspection brief unless permission is given to stay longer.
Allow the tenant to be present.
Repair any damage caused during the inspection. 
Always assess for safety risks during inspections. If the owner refuses to address safety issues, report this to your employer promptly.

## Section 2. The appraisal – Overview

Presenting an appraisal isn’t just about numbers—it's about building trust, explaining your evidence clearly, and managing expectations professionally
When you present your appraisal report, your goal is to: 
Demonstrate market knowledge 
	
Communicate value clearly 
	
Respond professionally to client expectations or objections
An appraisal report is more than a price estimate—it's a professional document that reflects your knowledge, diligence, and credibility as a real estate representative. 
A well-prepared report: 
builds trust with clients 
sets clear expectations 
protects you from legal risk and complaints 
can lead to future referrals when your appraisals prove accurate.
Your goal is to write an appraisal that is clear, justifiable, and risk-aware.

## 2.2 Timing and compliance

According to LGIRS, the written appraisal must be given to the client before they sign the Appointment to Act
Best practice: 
Include the completed appraisal page before requesting a signature.
	
Keep a copy of the signed appraisal report for your records. 
	
Ensure the client acknowledges receipt of the appraisal. 
If you don’t want to arrange a second meeting, you can: 
scan or photograph the signed appraisal page and email it from your phone 
leave the original with the client and keep a photo for your records. 
Even if you're appraising a property for a friend, family member, or repeat client, you must still provide a formal written appraisal if you express an opinion of value. 
Appraisal is not just a conversation—it’s a regulated professional service.

## 2.3 Meeting the client

Step-by-step
When meeting a prospective seller or landlord for the first time, focus on two key goals: 
Build rapport.
Gain their trust in your appraisal expertise. 
Suggested steps for new sales representatives 
When you're starting out, follow this structured approach: 
Greet the client warmly and introduce yourself and your agency. 


Reconfirm the purpose of the meeting (appraisal, advice, listing enquiry, etc.).


Ask questions to understand their situation (timing, goals, concerns).


Listen actively, acknowledge what they say, and clarify any assumptions. 


Present the CMA step by step—explain the data, not just the price. 


Discuss your price estimate and how it was reached. 


Invite questions and encourage discussion of their own price ideas. 


Explain next steps (e.g. preparing a listing agreement, follow-up actions.
Essential interpersonal skills 
Your market knowledge is critical—but so are your interpersonal skills. These skills help make your advice believable, professional, and respectful. 
Skill 
	
Why it matters 


Effective questioning 
	
Helps clarify the client’s motivations, plans, and expectations 


Active listening 
	
Shows you value their input—use nodding, eye contact, and summarising 


Tact and perception 
	
Allows you to highlight positives in the property without dismissing concerns 


Negotiation 
	
Helps you reach agreement on value, fees, or marketing approach 

Be prepared for client expectations 
It’s common for clients to mention: 
their own price expectations, or 
another agent’s appraisal.
If your appraisal result differs, be ready to explain your reasoning calmly and clearly, using: 
evidence from comparable sales or rentals
visuals from the CMA (charts, maps, property summaries) 
commentary about local market trends
Confidence backed by evidence helps reduce pushback. 
Prepare your appraisal presentation​ 
Before presenting an appraisal to a client: 
Review all report content for accuracy and relevance.
Practise the presentation with a colleague if possible.
Anticipate and prepare for common client questions (e.g. “Why did you choose this price range?”)

## 2.5 Completing the final appraisal page

Some CMA reports include an appraisal summary page, while others require you to add one before meeting the client
This page should include:
a summary of the property details
a field to write in the appraised price or rent range
supporting commentary that meets REBA Code Rule 26.
​ 
Rule 26 Requirements – REBA Code
When providing an opinion of market price, you must give the client:
A written statement of the price or rent opinion.
The reasons for that opinion.
If available, sales or rental evidence that supports it.
Property managers take note: While Rule 26 refers to sales, the best practice is for property managers to follow the same process when giving rental price opinions. 
​

## 2.6 Appraisal value

Be cautious with automated values 
While some representatives rely on the automated value generated by the CMA tool, best practice is to treat this only as a starting point. 
Why? 
Systems may include broadly similar properties (e.g. 3-bedroom homes) that are not truly comparable. 
Market conditions may have changed since the most recent sales. 
Some properties may have unique features or limitations not captured in the data. 
Always review the suggested value critically and adjust based on your own knowledge and inspection. 
​ 
Fine-tuning the appraisal value 
Use your judgement and local market insight to determine whether the subject property is likely to sell or rent for more, less, or similar to, the comparables in the CMA. 
​ 
Key factors that may require adjustment
Category 
	
What to consider 


Building condition 
	
Age, build quality, number of living areas, layout flexibility 


Interior quality 
	
Fixtures, fittings, kitchen and bathroom quality, floor coverings 


Sustainability 
	
Energy rating, solar panels, insulation, air conditioning 


Outdoor features 
	
Pools, sheds, bore, entertaining areas, mature gardens 


Desirability 
	
School zones, cafes, parks, public transport, travel time to city 


Location advantages 
	
Water or park views, northerly aspect, prestige street, proximity to beach or river 


Land characteristics 
	
Block size, gradient (flat vs steep), fencing, flood risk 


Negative influences 
	
Noise pollution, crime stigma, bushfire zoning, nearby industrial use 


Market changes 
	
Has the market risen or fallen since data was captured in the CMA? 
​ 
Sales-specific considerations 
For sales appraisals, also consider: 
Development potential (e.g. subdivision or zoning flexibility).
Limitations on title (e.g. encumbrances, easements, heritage listings).
‘Highest and best use’—what a valuer would consider as the optimal use of the site under planning regulations.

## 2.7 Reducing risk

Risk-reduction checklist: What to include
To ensure your appraisal is professional and defensible, cover the following points where relevant. 
Component 
	
Purpose 


1. Purpose of the appraisal 
	
State that the report estimates probable sale price or rental value at a point in time 


2. Method used 
	
e.g. Direct comparison using CMA data and analysis 


3. Market overview 
	
Include suburb-level info like price or rent trends, days on market, vacancy rates 


4. Property description 
	
Include key features—scale the detail based on the property’s type and value 


5. Data set used 
	
Specify source and scope, e.g. “Cotality sales, postcode 6010, between Jan–June 2025” 


6. Comparable properties 
	
List or summarise the 3+ key comparables used to support your opinion 


7. Estimated value range 
	
Provide a clearly defined price or rental range, not a single figure 


8. Validity period and disclaimers 
	
State how long the appraisal is valid (e.g. 30 or 60 days), and include any limiting conditions 


9. Special circumstances 
	
Note any seller/owner preferences (e.g. “quiet sale”, privacy restrictions, tenant retained) 


10. Assumptions 
	
e.g. “Sale expected via private treaty”; “Assumes property remains in current condition” 


11. Limitations 
	
Clarify any scope limits, e.g. no internal inspection yet, limited data, or market volatility 
​ 
	
Why this matters​ 
A clear, well-reasoned appraisal: 
protects your professional reputation 
helps prevent client misunderstandings 
demonstrates that you've followed best practice and REBA Code requirements 
makes your appraisal easy to follow and justify if ever questioned.
The reader should always be able to follow the logic of your pricing estimate—from the data, to the reasoning, to the final figure. 
​ 
Legal risk: Keep it realistic 
While lawsuits over appraisals are rare, complaints and loss of trust are common if: 
your estimate seems unfounded or exaggerated 
the client relies on your figure and feels misled 
market changes aren’t factored in or disclosed.
Best practice = realistic estimate + clear explanation + defined timeframe. 
​ 
Risk and risk management in appraisals 
To minimise legal and reputational risk: 
Do
	
Avoid


Inspect the property before giving an appraisal 
	
Giving verbal or ad hoc appraisals without proper diligence 


Confirm all appraisals in writing 
	
Assuming the client understands without clarification 


Be honest and evidence-based in all pricing 
	
Overstating value to win business 


Document and retain records of appraisal basis 
	
Relying solely on memory or informal notes 


Address and report any safety issues observed 
	
Ignoring potential hazards during inspection 
Legal reminder: You and your agency may be liable if harm comes to someone due to a safety risk you observed but failed to act on. 
For more detailed strategies, refer to your Risk Management unit. 
​ 
Legal and compliance notes
All selling fees and reimbursable costs must be documented in the Appointment to Act. 
Dollar amounts must be initialled by the client. 
If fees or costs change during the campaign, use a formal variation document signed by all original parties. 
You can only claim what you spend — and must factor in any rebates received. 
Reminder: Misleading a client about your success or overpromising to secure a listing breaches consumer law and the REBA Act.

## Section 3. Socio-economic and political factors – Overview

Do socio-economic and political factors affect appraisals?​ 
While most appraisals are based on local, comparable property data, broader socio-economic and political factors can influence value trends and may explain why prices or rents rise or fall in a particular market. 
Your role as a real estate professional is to stay informed, interpret current conditions, and communicate factual trends clearly. While you should avoid predictions, you can use this context to help clients understand market conditions. 
​

## 3.1 Demographics and demand

Shifts in population age or household type can influence demand

Impact on property values​ 


Aging population 
	
Demand for smaller, low-maintenance homes increases; demand for large homes may decline 


Young families moving into an area 
	
Increased interest in family-sized homes; proximity to schools becomes a value driver

## 3.2 Interest rates

Interest rates affect borrowing costs, which in turn impact property prices 
Rising interest rates → higher mortgage repayments → reduced buyer demand → price pressure down.
Falling interest rates → cheaper borrowing → increased demand → upward pressure on prices.
Changes in interest rates may not impact all suburbs equally—some areas may remain more resilient depending on local demand and affordability.

## 3.3 Government policy and legislation

Policy decisions at state or federal level can have short- and long-term effects on prices or rents 


Policy examples​ 
	
Potential impact​

Short-term boost in demand from eligible buyers

​

## 3.4 Economic cycles and sector impacts

The broader economy also influences property trends—but not always equally across all regions or property types
A slowing economy typically reduces confidence and delays purchasing decisions.
Industry-specific downturns (e.g. mining) often hit hardest in localised areas before impacting the wider market.
Example: When the WA mining boom slowed, prices declined first in mining towns and resource-linked metro areas. Over time, the broader Perth market felt the effects too.

## 5.7 Rental vs sales CMAs

Same principles, different application 
Although this example is based on a sales CMA, the same principles apply when comparing properties for rent. 
Key point: Compare like with like—the more recent and similar the comparable properties, the more accurate your rental appraisal will be. 
Appraising rentals: What property managers consider
Just like sales representatives, property managers: 
compare quality, features, and location 
review current listings in the area 
position the subject property within a market-based price range.
Why getting the price right matters 
Setting the right rental price is critical. If a property is priced too high: 
it may sit vacant longer than necessary 
the owner could lose weeks of income, especially in a quiet market. 
For example, if a property sits vacant for 4 extra weeks, that could represent a 5% loss of the owner’s annual rental income. 
The same risk applies in sales: Overpricing may lead to a property going stale on the market.
This often results in discounting and a lower final sale price than if priced correctly from the start.
