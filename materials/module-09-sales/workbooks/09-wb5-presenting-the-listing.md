# Presenting the Listing

## Section 1. Listing presentation kit – Overview

The listing presentation kit is what you take to the seller meeting
It helps you clearly present your offer, back up your appraisal, and show the seller why you’re the best choice. 
You might use: 
a laptop/tablet for a dynamic presentation 
printed materials to leave with the client.
​ 
Your goal: Make it easy for the seller to understand your service, feel confident in your ability, and say yes to listing. 
A professional listing kit: 
helps the seller feel informed and supported
shows that you’re organised, skilled, and ready to work
helps you stand out from competing agents
creates a lasting impression—even after the meeting ends.

## 1.2 The Comparative Market Analysis (CMA)

Plan the listing presentation with the appraisal as the centrepiece
Your listing presentation is your opportunity to win the seller’s confidence and secure the listing. At the heart of this presentation is your Comparative Market Analysis (CMA)—the document that shows you understand the market, have done your homework, and can price the property realistically and professionally. 
​ 
Why the CMA matters​ 
Your CMA will likely be the main focus for the seller. It’s also now a legal requirement if you intend to offer an opinion about the property’s market value. 
This means your CMA must be: 
accurate 
transparent 
clearly explained 
based on relevant and recent data.
	
​ 
What a strong CMA should include 
Your CMA should provide a complete and compelling market snapshot that supports your appraisal. At a minimum, it should contain: 
CMA component​ 
	
Purpose​ 


Summary of recent sales in the area 
	
Shows the general market trend and buyer behaviour 


Details of 3 comparable sold properties 
	
Supports your value estimate with similar, recent sales 


Summary of competing active listings 
	
Helps the client understand their competition 


Details of 3 comparable current listings 
	
Shows how their property stacks up in today’s market 


Details of any withdrawn listings 
	
Gives insight into overpricing or poor marketing outcomes 


Sales trend and suburb statistics 
	
Adds depth and context—days on market, median prices, etc. 


Area map with sold/for sale properties highlighted 
	
Makes it easier for the client to visualise market activity 


Image of the subject property 
	
Adds professionalism and helps personalise the report 


Recommended price range and listing price 
	
Required if offering a market opinion (in line with current legal standards) 
​ 
Case study 1: Winning trust with a clear CMA​ 
​ 
Scenario
A new sales representative presented a CMA with clear comparables, maps, and a summary that explained her reasoning in simple language. The sellers said her clarity and professionalism made it easy to choose her over another agent who had given them only a vague estimate. 
Learning point: Sellers value clarity and evidence-based reasoning over flashy promises. 
How to elevate your CMA
Why this approach works  
Good practice: Never guarantee a sale price—but always support your estimate with solid evidence and clear explanations. 
​ 
Case study 2: The power of professionalism​ 
Scenario
An experienced agent arrived at a listing appointment with a neatly prepared kit, testimonials, and a clear communication plan. The sellers commented that while another agent had promised a higher price, this agent’s professionalism and preparation gave them more confidence. They signed the agreement that same day.
Learning point
Sellers often choose the agent who inspires trust—not the one who inflates price expectations.

## Section 2. Six steps to presenting a property appraisal effectively – Overview

Presenting an appraisal is more than just quoting a price—it’s about connection, credibility, and communication
The following six-step process will help you build trust with your client and present your appraisal professionally and persuasively.

### Page images

- ![REIWA - WIDE - 2025-11-10T100539.471.png](https://fs-prd.axcelerate.com.au/images/66949502/REIWA%20-%20WIDE%20-%202025-11-10T100539.471.png)

## 2.1 Step 1: Ask questions

Start the conversation by inviting the client to talk
Open-ended questions are key—they show interest and help build rapport.
Use gentle, conversational prompts with an “empathy cushion”:
“The house is in great condition—have you made improvements since you bought?"
“This looks like a wonderful family home—was that your focus when you moved in?”
“Tell me about anything happening locally that might help our campaign.”
	
If some questions were answered previously (e.g. by phone), refer back to those responses instead of repeating them. This shows you were listening.​

### Page images

- ![licensed-image (2).jpg](https://fs-prd.axcelerate.com.au/images/66949502/licensed-image%20(2).jpg)

## 2.2 Step 2: Clarify answers

Not all answers will be clear or complete—some sellers take time to open up
Use active listening to summarise and clarify: “So you’re hoping to move into something more compact—ideally by October?”
Clarifying builds trust and ensures you understand the seller’s timeline and priorities.

### Page images

- ![REIWA - WIDE (2).png](https://fs-prd.axcelerate.com.au/images/66949502/E7bYsEG6XH51cGq4HJ2leQ9tn/REIWA%20-%20WIDE%20(2).png)

## 2.3 Step 3: Highlight positives and find common ground

You may not always agree on price or strategy
That’s okay—but if you’ve established common ground, the conversation is more likely to stay constructive.
To strengthen rapport:
show genuine appreciation for the property
express interest in the seller’s next steps
look for shared experiences or values.
People are more likely to work with “PLUs” – People Like Us.
​

### Page images

- ![REIWA - WIDE (3).png](https://fs-prd.axcelerate.com.au/images/66949502/REIWA%20-%20WIDE%20(3).png)

## 2.4 Step 4: Suggest solutions

If the appraisal figure isn't what the seller expected, be ready to respond positively and professionally
Examples of solutions:
Explain any market shifts since neighbouring properties sold.
Suggest ways to boost appeal (e.g. professional photos, staging).
Reframe concerns using the “Feel–Felt–Found” technique: “I understand you feel hesitant about extra marketing. One seller I worked with felt the same way. They found that a small investment in professional photos made all the difference—and the property sold quickly.”
Use real, relatable examples to build confidence.

### Page images

- ![REIWA - WIDE (4).png](https://fs-prd.axcelerate.com.au/images/66949502/REIWA%20-%20WIDE%20(4).png)

## 2.5 Step 5: Offer choices

People like to feel in control
Offer options that help sellers move toward a decision:
Suggest a formal valuation for high-value or hard-to-appraise homes.
Offer an ‘Expressions of Interest’ campaign to test the market.
Provide tiered marketing packages (e.g. basic vs premium with video/staging).
Recommend a building inspection report if repairs are visible.
	
You can either assume the listing is coming your way, or phrase your suggestions gently: “If you choose to work with me, I’d suggest we start with ...”

## 2.6 Step 6: Encourage decisions without pressure

Some sellers need time to process—don’t rush them
Instead:
Get agreement on small points first, and build from there.
Share testimonials or case studies to build credibility.
If emotions are involved (e.g. a relationship breakdown), show empathy: “This must be a hard time. Would it help to move forward with the sale so you can focus on what’s next?”
Ask if other agents have appraised the property and what values they suggested. This can help you understand the seller’s mindset and reinforce your credibility.

The most effective appraisal conversations balance market expertise with genuine connection
Listen deeply, explain clearly, and tailor your message to each client’s unique needs.

## Section 3. Face-to-face meeting – Overview

Clarifying the seller’s needs, motivations and priorities
When you meet a prospective seller face-to-face, it’s your chance to go deeper than any initial phone call or email. Thoughtful questioning helps you uncover the real reasons behind the sale—and these insights can shape your approach, the timing, and even the appraised value.

### Page images

- ![REIWA - WIDE - 2025-11-10T100712.433.png](https://fs-prd.axcelerate.com.au/images/66949502/REIWA%20-%20WIDE%20-%202025-11-10T100712.433.png)

## 3.1 Go beyond the basics

Use this meeting to confirm and expand on what you already know
Some questions you might ask include:
“Has your ideal timeline for selling changed since we last spoke?”
“Do you have a preferred method of sale—or would you like to discuss the options together?”
“Have you purchased elsewhere, or are you still deciding?”
“What kind of marketing do you feel most comfortable with?”
“How would you prefer we stay in touch—email, phone, or text?”
“Have you had appraisals from other agents? What were your thoughts?”
These questions build rapport, uncover expectations, and show you’re listening.

### Page images

- ![REIWA - WIDE (7).png](https://fs-prd.axcelerate.com.au/images/66949502/REIWA%20-%20WIDE%20(7).png)

## 3.2 Understand the motivation behind the sale

People sell for all kinds of reasons—some clear, others more private
Try to read between the lines while remaining tactful. Here are some potential motivations:
Lifestyle changes
Personal factors
Financial needs
Relocation
Property-related issues
​ 
Try soft openers like:
“What made you choose this time to sell?”
	
“Will you miss the neighbourhood?”
	
“Have you started looking at other properties yet?”
​ 
Sensitive motivations or hidden issues
If someone seems guarded, it may indicate:
an emotional or sensitive reason behind the sale
a property issue they’re hesitant to mention (e.g. repairs, stigma)
uncertainty about whether to proceed.
Be supportive but professional. If you suspect urgency or a stigmatised property, gently explore further. These factors may influence your appraisal and should be reflected in your discussion.

## Section 4. Delivering your appraisal presentation – Overview

The ultimate goal of your appraisal presentation is to secure the listing and demonstrate how you will achieve the best possible outcome for the client
This requires preparation, professionalism, and the ability to build trust quickly—even if you've been referred.
​ 
Be ready to win the business
You may only get one chance to impress, so arrive:
with current market insights and comparative data
carrying all required agency documentation
prepared to explain and justify your appraisal and marketing approach.
Tip: It’s best to have all legal owners present for your listing presentation, as all must sign the authority to act. Explain this politely if only one owner is available.
​

## 4.1 Build rapport, maintain control

Adopt a confident, engaging style while allowing space for the seller to ask questions
Keep the discussion flowing by:
adapting your communication style to match the seller’s tone and pace
avoiding industry jargon and using plain language
listening actively: summarise their responses to show you’re tuned in
maintaining a calm, professional tone—especially if challenged
reinforcing your expertise with relevant past results or success stories
demonstrating genuine interest in their property and their goals.
Choose a space in the seller’s home where your materials can be reviewed comfortably and without distraction.

## 4.3 Key points to cover

When presenting your appraisal
Explain how the CMA method works, including how direct comparison underpins the estimated price.
Discuss comparable sold properties highlighting style, size, condition, and recent sale prices.
Clarify the source of data, e.g. Cotality, Pricefinder, Landgate.
Interpret current market trends, and whether prices are trending up, down or remaining steady.
Show competing listings, so the seller understands their current competition.
Tip: Point out which comparable sales you believe are most relevant to their home and why.

## 4.4 Address expectations

Some sellers may overestimate their property’s value due to emotional attachment or past market highs
Be empathetic, but realistic:
A beautiful garden may be a passion project for the seller, but not every buyer will see it as added value.
A designer home with two bedrooms may appeal to a niche market but miss broader buyer demand.
Use the CMA as an objective tool to gently educate the seller about how buyers are likely to perceive the property.

## 4.7 Build a price range

Use comparables
Use two or three well-chosen comparables to set realistic expectations. For example:
“76 Smith St is similar but less modern—it sold for $540,000 a few months ago when the market was slower.”
“10 Smith St is newer, larger and sold last week for $580,000. Yours sits between the two.”
Show photos, walk through key differences, and back it up with CMA data.
	
​ 
Narrow the range and set expectations
Help clients understand where their home fits in the current market.
For example:
“8 Brown St is listed at $550,000—it’s similar but only has one bathroom. 24 Jones St is at $590,000, but it has three bathrooms and a pool, so it’s in a different league.”
Then relate the evidence back to the seller’s property and the market trend:
“Demand is picking up. With your property’s appeal, I’d expect interest in the $550,000–$570,000 range. How does that align with your expectations?”
​ 
Keep it collaborative
Bring the client into the process.
Ask for their views.
Address any emotional attachment to features with empathy and evidence.
Be transparent about pricing risk if expectations are too high.
Use the CMA as a live tool: flip through it with the seller, reference comparable images, and review online listings together if needed. This helps bridge the gap between perception and market reality.

## Section 5. Secure the listing – Overview

Time to list the property
Now that the appraisal is complete and your client is ready to proceed, it’s time to move into the listing stage. At this point, you’ll:
discuss the most suitable method of sale.
step through the terms and conditions of the selling agreement
negotiate fees and charges in line with agency policy
complete the Selling Agency Agreement
develop a clear communication plan.
You’ll also be responsible for preparing a compliant contract that meets current legislative requirements and aligns with agency standards.

## 5.1 Gain commitment and handle questions

Revisit the seller’s motivation
As you move to secure the listing, it’s important to revisit the seller’s motivation.
Properties with motivated sellers are often the most marketable — and understanding their goals will help you guide the process more effectively.

Ask about price expectations
If their figure aligns with the market, you can confirm and support it. If not, use your Comparative Market Analysis (CMA) to open a discussion around market value, while respectfully acknowledging that the final price decision is theirs.
Sometimes, sellers will want to pause to reflect or consult privately — allow space for this.
​ 
Be the trusted expert
Sellers are looking to you for insight. Use your CMA and local knowledge to answer questions confidently and clearly, including:
current pricing and demand trends in their segment of the market
activity levels and the competitiveness of their property type
strategic advice for positioning and selling their property effectively.
They may also ask more detailed questions. Such as:
your track record and results — have testimonials ready
the structure of agency fees, marketing costs and what’s included
your take on selling methods and which approach suits their goals
the current competition in the market, and how to stand out
recent sales and how those outcomes impact their appraisal. 
​ 
Explain marketing and fees
Always be transparent about marketing options. Focus on what will genuinely help their campaign — don’t upsell unnecessarily. Show how strong, targeted marketing can drive buyer interest and boost the final sale price.
If fees come up early, don’t sidestep the question — answer with clarity and confidence in your value. Be ready to break down:
what’s included in your selling fee
the difference between marketing packages (e.g. basic vs premium ads)
examples of how quality photos, video, or social media campaigns can enhance buyer engagement.
	​ 
​ 
Tailor your advice and presentation to their needs and focus on building trust — the key to a successful listing agreement.

## 5.2 Negotiate the selling fee, charges and expenses

When a licensed agent sells a property or business, they typically charge a commission — often a percentage of the sale price
It's common for sellers to try to negotiate this fee, especially in competitive markets.
​ 
Positioning your value
Rather than rushing to discount, focus on the value you deliver. Confidently explain what sets you (and your agency) apart. Discount only if necessary — for instance, to secure a high-value listing or win a client during a slow patch. If you do reduce your fee, consider negotiating a higher marketing spend to boost exposure and reduce time on market.

Tactics for fee discussions
Approach fee negotiations with confidence and professionalism. Here are some effective strategies:
Showcase success
Demonstrate value
Performance-driven fees
Strength in negotiation
Break it down
Back up your position with data, recent campaigns, and performance evidence — always honestly and accurately.
​ 
When an agency may lose the right to charge a fee
Under the Real Estate and Business Agents Act 1978 (WA), there are specific circumstances where an agency may forfeit the right to claim commission or fees. It’s critical to know these and avoid missteps that can lead to disputes or loss of income.
​ 
The REBA Act requirements (summarised) include:
s.60. Agent not entitled to commission etc. unless licensed and validly appointed
s.61. Maximum remuneration of licensees, fixing of etc.
s.64. Conflicts of interest of agents etc.
​ 
The REBA Regulations requirements include:
r.6BA. Appointment to act as agent, content of
The Appointment to Act must clearly set out the amount of the selling fee, or how it will be calculated, with an example. A maximum amount payable must be shown as a dollar amount. It must list services to be delivered, and expenses to be reimbursed. Advice about dispute resolution via the Commissioner for Consumer Protection is required.
​ 
The REBA Code requirements include:
r.13. Fiduciary obligations
r.17. Appointment to Act
r.18. Restriction on discounts and rebates
r.19. Further restriction on reward for service
r.20. Claiming expenses from client
r.26. Duties regarding opinion of market price
Note:​ A representative must be registered and employed by a licensed agent to earn commission.

## 5.3 Methods of sale

What to explain to sellers
When listing a property, it’s essential to clearly explain the available methods of sale and how each process works. This helps the seller make an informed decision and allows you to tailor the marketing strategy and listing documentation accordingly.
​ 
When is this conversation held?
During the listing presentation, before the Appointment to Act is signed. The marketing plan may be finalised later, but the method of sale and budget should be agreed upfront.

Overview of sale methods
Private treaty (offer and acceptance)
Expression of interest (EOI) / Set date sale
Public auction
Sale by tender
​ 
Final notes
As the sales representative, it's your role to explain:
how each method works
the costs and timelines involved
which method best suits the property, the market, and the client’s goals.
Tip: Tailor your recommendation to the seller’s motivation, timeframe, and property type.

Scenario: Learning from a lost listing
After completing a detailed listing presentation for Peter and Mel, owners of a well-maintained three-bedroom home in Fantasy Glen, you feel confident. You provided a clear Comparative Market Analysis (CMA), explained recent market trends, outlined your proposed marketing strategy, and answered their questions about selling fees and timeframes. The meeting went well, and you left optimistic.
A few days later, Peter calls to thank you for your time. He explains that they’ve chosen to list with another agent. Instead of ending the call there, you respond professionally and ask if they’d be open to sharing any feedback about your presentation.
Peter says:
“We really liked your approach and your CMA was very clear. But the other agent showed us a live demo of their online listing tool and gave us a really structured weekly communication plan. We just felt they had more systems in place to keep us informed.”
Though disappointed, you thank him for the honest feedback and assure him you’re always available if they need a second opinion or market update down the track.
That evening, you take a moment to reflect. The feedback helps you identify two key areas for improvement: enhancing the visual delivery of your marketing tools, and strengthening your communication plan. You decide to revise your presentation kit to include clearer examples of your weekly reporting system and pre-prepared marketing visuals. You also schedule a follow-up for yourself to check in with Peter and Mel in six weeks—just in case they decide to reappraise their decision.

Why it matters
	
Sometimes, feedback comes after you don’t secure the listing—and that’s okay. These moments can be some of the most valuable learning opportunities. Analysing what the client appreciated and what influenced their final decision allows you to refine your future presentations and client communication strategy.
Just as importantly, maintaining professionalism and staying in touch—even when you don’t win the business—can position you for future opportunities. 
Vendors often change their minds, refer friends and family, or come back to you down the track when your professionalism leaves a lasting impression.

## 5.8 Understanding listing agreements and their purpose

Regardless of the method of sale chosen, a valid appointment to act (commonly referred to as a listing agreement) is required before any selling activity begins

Under the REBA Act, it is a breach to represent a client without this written agreement in place. The agreement, also known as a listing authority or authority to act, must:
be signed by the person receiving the service (or a legally authorised signatory)
clearly outline the services to be provided
include the licensed agent’s full details
specify the length of the authority period
include the intended listing price, if known
identify the property being listed (if for a specific property)
set out how commission or fees will be calculated, including:
a clear example of the commission calculation
a statement confirming that commission rates are negotiable, not fixed
the maximum payable amount in dollars (if commission is percentage-based)
the maximum payable amount in dollars (if an hourly rate is used).
​ 
​ 
The REBA Code Rule 25. Client identification verification states:
An agent or sales representative who receives instructions to offer real estate for sale must:
promptly obtain a copy of the certificate of title for the real estate and refer to that copy for the name of the registered proprietor; and
as soon as practicable after receiving the instructions and before a contract for that sale is executed, make all reasonable efforts to verify -
the identity of each person who claims to be, or to act for, a person who is to sell all or any of the real estate; and
each person’s authority to sell the real estate, or to act for the person selling it.
​ 
Signing the authority to act
All legal owners listed on the Certificate of Title must sign the appointment to act (listing agreement). If someone else is signing on their behalf, they must hold a registered Power of Attorney (POA) specifically for the sale of land. In WA, this POA must be registered with Landgate. Agents are responsible for confirming that the POA is correctly registered before acting on it and must retain a copy on file.
​ 
Types of listing agreements
The type of listing agreement used depends on how the seller wants to market their property and whether they want to give the agency exclusive rights or retain flexibility.
Type of agreement
	
Exclusive Listing Agreement (REIWA Form 109)
	
Non-Exclusive (Open) Listing Agreement (REIWA Form 102)


Agency rights
	
Agency has exclusive rights to market and sell the property during the listing period
	
Property may be listed with multiple agencies or sold privately by the owner


Commission
	
Seller must pay the agreed fee if the property is sold during the listing period—even if the seller or another agent finds the buyer
	
Fee is only payable if the agent introduces the successful buyer


Conjunctional arrangements
	
Allowed at the agency’s discretion and cost
	
Also allowed, at the listing agent’s discretion and cost


Seller suitability
	
Best for sellers who want a single point of contact and a committed agent
	
Suitable for sellers who want flexibility or to try selling privately


Agent advantage
	
Encourages investment in marketing and buyer engagement
	
Less control and higher risk of missed commission unless the agent secures the buyer
​ 
Auction listing agreement
If the property is to be sold via auction, the REIWA Auction Agreement is used. Under this agreement, the seller:
grants the agency the right to sell the property at auction on a specified date
agrees to pay the fee if the property sells before, at, or after auction (within the agreed exclusive period)
may not avoid paying the fee by using another agency during the listing term.
​ 
Negotiating terms of the listing agreement
Every listing agreement includes terms that can be negotiated between the agent and the seller. These might include:
Marketing costs
Reimbursement clauses
Other arrangements
​ 
Supplying a true copy of the agreement
Take care: In compliance with the REBA Code of Conduct, each client must be given a true copy of the signed listing authority at the time it is signed.
This is a legal requirement. Failing to provide a true copy may invalidate the authority or expose the agency to penalties.

​ 
Anti-Money Laundering and Counter-Terrorism Financing (AML/CTF)
As part of AML/CTF compliance, onboarding and customer due diligence (CDD) must be followed carefully and consistently. 
Onboarding is the process of collecting and verifying key client information before providing services.
CDD involves: 
confirming identity
understanding the nature and purpose of the relationship
assessing risk
monitoring for changes or suspicious activity. 
Following legislation and office procedures is essential. It helps staff: 
identify red flags early
maintain accurate records
meet legal obligations
reduce the risk of being used for unlawful activity.

## 6.1 Disclosure obligations

Under Section 64 of the REBA Act and the REBA Code of Conduct
 You are legally required to make written disclosure to the seller if:
you recommend a service provider (e.g. settlement agent, photographer) where you or your agency have a financial or significant relationship with that provider
there is a conflict of interest between your role and the seller’s interests
you, or someone close to you (a relative, colleague, or associate) have a direct or indirect personal interest in the transaction—for example, if you want to buy or rent the property.
	
​ 
The REBA Code of Conduct Rule 18 Restriction on discounts and rebates
An agent or sales representative must not, in connection with a transaction or a service provided to a client by the agent or sales representative, demand or receive a discount or rebate that relates to a service provided by a service provider, unless the agent or sales representative has—
disclosed to the client the full nature, extent and amount of the discount or rebate; and
obtained the written consent of the client to the demanding or receiving of the discount or rebate by the agent or sales representative.

## 6.2 Beneficial interest and disclosure obligations

As an agent or sales representative, you have a fiduciary duty to act in your client’s best interests
If you (or someone close to you) are personally interested in buying, leasing, or otherwise benefiting from a listed property, this creates a serious conflict of interest.
​ 
Acting with a beneficial interest
If you wish to purchase or lease a property listed with your agency (for yourself, a relative, or close associate), you must:
make full written disclosure of your interest
obtain the client’s informed written consent before proceeding
advise the client that they are not obligated to pay any commission or fee related to the transaction.
A common tool for this is REIWA’s Form 117 Disclosure of Interest and Consent in Real Estate, or equivalent documentation.
​ 
Disclosure to buyers – REBA Code Rule 8
If you’re representing a seller and have a family or financial relationship with them, you must also disclose this to prospective buyers:
Written disclosure must be made to every purchaser if you are related to the vendor.
A sales representative or agent is considered related to the vendor if they:
hold a controlling interest in the vendor entity, or
are a spouse, de facto partner, child, grandchild, sibling, parent, or grandparent of the vendor.

## 6.3 Material facts and required disclosures

As a sales representative, you are legally required to disclose any material facts to prospective buyers
These are facts that could influence a buyer’s decision to purchase the property or impact the price they are willing to pay.
Examples of material facts include:
Structural defects (e.g. cracking, foundation issues).
Termite damage or infestation.
Drainage problems or flood risk.
Noise disturbances (e.g. aircraft flight paths or nearby venues).
Stigma issues (e.g. a property known for past criminal activity).
If you are uncertain whether something is a material fact, err on the side of caution and disclose it—or seek advice from your licensee or agency principal.
​ 
Seller disclosure statements
While not mandatory for most sales, seller disclosure statements are becoming a common best-practice tool in residential sales. They are required by law for Strata Title properties but can also be used for freehold sales to reduce risk and promote transparency.
These statements should be completed and signed by the seller, outlining any material facts known about the property, including issues such as structural defects, encumbrances, or building approvals.
	
Using a seller disclosure statement:
helps ensure the seller takes responsibility for the information provided
protects the agency and sales representative, particularly if it includes a disclaimer stating that the information is provided in good faith and not warranted by the agency.
The REIWA Form 322 Seller’s Disclosure Statement is recommended for general use where a disclosure document is to be provided.

## 6.4 ATO clearance certificate

Vendor requirement
Important: Sellers must obtain a clearance certificate from the Australian Taxation Office (ATO) before settlement.
If the seller does not provide this certificate the buyer is legally required to withhold 15% of the purchase price at settlement.
This amount is paid directly to the ATO, in accordance with foreign resident capital gains withholding rules.
Ensure sellers are made aware of this early in the sales process to avoid delays at settlement.

## 6.5 Title and property interest reports

A title search is a legal requirement for every property listed for sale
This search confirms ownership and reveals any encumbrances, such as easements, covenants or mortgages, all of which must be disclosed to prospective buyers.
Best practice is also to provide buyers with a Landgate Property Interest Report, which outlines a broader range of interests that may affect the property such as heritage status, contamination notices, or road widening proposals.
Buyers should be asked to sign an acknowledgement confirming they have received this report.

## 6.7 Documentation

Some verbal discussions with buyers may carry legal or financial implications
Consider documenting conversations if any of the following occur
The buyer discusses a proposed use of the property (e.g. subdividing, adding a pool, building a granny flat).
You clarify or correct assumptions about zoning, structural condition, council approvals, or utilities.
The buyer inquires about timber pests, renovations, or the potential to redevelop.
You mention or the buyer asks about future development, subdivision potential, or infrastructure changes that may affect value.
When recording
Note the date, time, and method of conversation (e.g. phone, in-person).
Include the name of the buyer and any specific questions asked.
Record your responses, including any disclaimers or referrals (e.g. “I suggested they check with local council regarding development potential”).
	
When appropriate, send a summary email to the buyer confirming the discussion. This ensures clarity and can help mitigate future risk.
​ 
Keeping records
Throughout the appraisal and listing process, you must ensure that agency systems are accurately updated, including:
The client sales file
	
The central agency CRM or database
	
Your personal recordkeeping system, if used
Verbal agreements and key discussions should always be confirmed in writing—preferably via email—to create a clear paper trail in case of any future disputes or complaints.
​ 
Documentation and compliance
Accurate documentation is essential, especially during the appraisal process. By this stage, you should have:
Created a client record, including title search results and zoning or planning checks.
Retained a written copy of the appraisal provided to the client.
Ensured this written appraisal complies with REBA Code Rule 26 (i.e., includes the opinion of market price, the reasons for that opinion, and supporting evidence).
Kept evidence that the appraisal was delivered to the client prior to signing the Appointment to Act.
These practices help demonstrate diligence and compliance with legislative obligations.

## 6.8 When clients aren’t happy

Recommended course of action
If a client is dissatisfied with the conduct of a real estate sales representative, they should be advised that the Department of Local Government, Industry Regulation and Safety (LGIRS) recommends the following course of action:
Raise the issue directly with the agency or representative in the first instance.
If unresolved, submit a written complaint to the agency’s licensee or principal.
If the matter remains unresolved or serious misconduct is suspected, the client may:
lodge a complaint with Consumer Protection (WA) via LGIRS
call the Consumer Protection Advice Line for guidance on 1300 30 40 54
submit a formal complaint online via the WA Government Consumer Protection website.
	
Representatives must handle complaints professionally and record all correspondence and outcomes as part of agency record-keeping obligations.

## Lesson 7.1 Use client feedback to improve your presentations

Even when you don’t secure the listing, there's still immense value in reflecting on the experience and what you can learn from the client's feedback

Why feedback matters
It gives you insights into how clients perceive your communication style, CMA, and value proposition.
	
It helps identify areas where you can build more confidence, clarity, or credibility.
	
Over time, feedback contributes to sharper, more effective listing presentations—and better results.
How to request feedback
Ask respectfully after the decision has been made.
Frame it as a learning opportunity:
“I really appreciated the opportunity to meet with you. If you have a moment, I’d love to know if there’s anything I could improve in my presentation or service.”
Avoid defensiveness—just listen.
​ 
How to analyse it
Use a simple reflection framework:
Question
	
Example response


What feedback was given?
	
“The CMA was clear, but they preferred the other agent’s marketing plan.”


What worked well?
	
“They liked my professionalism and communication style.”


What could be improved?
	
“Include a pricing strategy discussion, not just a range.”


What will I change for next time?
	
“Add marketing comparisons to my kit and rehearse that segment.”
​ 
Keep a feedback log
Document feedback patterns to spot trends. Over time, you’ll see what consistently works and what needs refining.
